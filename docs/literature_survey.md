# Literature and State-of-the-Art Survey

## Research area

This project studies runtime safety verification for Vision-Language-Action driving planners. The focus is not on training a driving model from scratch. Instead, we investigate whether a VLA-based supervisor can evaluate a candidate trajectory, selectively gather additional visual evidence, and intervene only when calibrated risk is high. The first phase studies only the tool-choice part, and calibration, veto and replanning are later phases (see [`refined_proposal.md`](refined_proposal.md)).

## SOTA observations

Recent work establishes several important capabilities:

1. VLA models can generate interpretable reasoning traces and driving actions.
2. Counterfactual reasoning can improve planning by allowing the model to revise unsafe behavior before execution.
3. Critics and verifiers can score and refine candidate trajectories (Judge, Then Drive, DriveJudge, DriveVer). In RRDrive the oracle-best candidate ranks 33.9th on average under the baseline scorer, and 27.7th after the scorer is given a risk profile for each candidate, so a large gap to the best candidate remains.
4. Tool-using driving agents have multiplied (DriveAgent-R1, AgentThink, MTRDrive, OmniDrive-R1, Drive by Hindsight and Foresight). None that we found conditions the tool choice on the candidate trajectories or reports accuracy against a query budget. Drive by Hindsight and Foresight has 27 tools and calls at least one on 99.5% of queries.
5. Tool calls are not automatically useful. The Illusion of Visual Tool-Use audits six models and finds two failure modes. In one, the returned observation has no causal effect on the answer, and the gain is concentrated in a calibrated minority of calls. In ToolGate, 78.3% of calls leave the immediate answer unchanged and 9.9% change it from right to wrong.
6. Many adaptive driving systems decide how much to think, not what to look at (AdaDrive and others). None of them chooses a perception query.
7. Triggers are moving from uncertainty to decision value. SIGMA, DEEP, the off-road paper 2611.06702, BLUE and ASSCG score a query or a mode switch by its effect on the plan. BLUE reports that scene complexity and kinematic features struggle to predict when language helps. Outside driving, Calibration Is Not Control lowers regret from 0.513 to 0.094 on ALFWorld by changing only the training target to intervention value. Training on the gain of a query is established practice (Calibrate-Then-Delegate, To Call or Not to Call, AdaDrive).
8. More evidence can hurt, and learned selectors are imperfect. In DEEP's monocular-geometry settings, 34% to 54% of escalations that change the loss make it worse. Learned allocators capture more benefit than random, about a third of the oracle's beneficial value, but select nearly as much harm as random.
9. Adding information to a candidate scorer is already studied: RRDrive compares scorers that see geometry, risk or both. The one precedent we found that chooses a sensing action from a planner's candidate set is the off-road paper 2611.06702, which is rule-based and not a driving system. We did not find a per-decision choice of which information to acquire, under a budget, in driving.
10. Sensor degradation and long-tail hazards can cause reasoning instability and dangerous planning errors (Lost in Fog). A tool helps only if its output can change the ranker's choice.
11. Related work points the same way as our weak prompted-VLM result, but none of it tests a VLM choosing a perception tool: DriveJudge scores trajectories, and the text-only study "LLM Agents Already Know When to Call Tools" does not use images.
12. Conformal and risk-calibrated methods exist for driving monitors (Localized Conformal Safety Monitoring) and for tool-using agents (Proof-of-Perception, Calibrate-Then-Delegate). In the papers we found, they are not combined with a driving planner that also chooses among perception tools.

## Gap addressed by this project

Prior work has answered several nearby questions:

- Can a VLA produce a better trajectory, or reflect on one it has produced?
- Can a critic or verifier score and re-rank candidate trajectories?
- Can a driving agent call a visual tool, and how much should it think before acting?
- Can a system decide whether an extra query or step is worth its cost by what it does to the plan, instead of by how uncertain the scene looks?
- Can a risk monitor or a call threshold be calibrated?

The fourth question is no longer open. SIGMA, DEEP, BLUE and ASSCG all score an expensive step by its effect on planning, and the off-road paper 2611.06702 does so for a planner's candidate rollouts. Several of these papers also show that extra evidence can hurt and that scene uncertainty is a weak trigger. Training a value head on the gain of a query is also established. We cite this work as prior art and do not claim it.

In the papers we found, one question remains open. Existing triggers make a single call-or-not decision, or choose among sensing actions without learning, outside driving. RRDrive shows that giving a scorer more information about each candidate helps, but it does not choose which information to acquire. We did not find a driving system that chooses among several perception tools from how a planner's candidate trajectories diverge, and none that scores that choice as accuracy against a query budget next to scene-uncertainty, random and oracle selectors. This is an absence over the papers we found, not over the field. Some of the closest work is under review and could be read only as abstracts.

PlanDiscrim, the first phase of CASS-VLA, asks this question:

> We study which perception query a driving planner should make, given its candidate trajectories. A learned selector chooses among ROI inspection, depth and a collision check, or abstains. It uses features of how the candidates diverge and is trained on each tool's decision gain. Averaged over query budgets, it beats the same learner without candidate features, ranker margin and random selection. It is evaluated in hazard-injected MetaDrive at three tool-noise levels, against an oracle selector and a fair ceiling.

The biggest open risk is the size of the candidate-conditioning gain. Our own results show five limits:

- The selector leads the scene-only MLP by only 1 to 2 points on average with 10,329 training rows, and 2 to 3 points with 25,036.
- It falls behind that rival at 0.95 and 1.00 calls per decision at mid noise, and at 1.00 with high noise or blocked ROI.
- It falls behind on `occluded_cross_traffic` on the final split at half a call per decision (0.330 against 0.347). Trained on 510 seeds (25,036 rows), on the test split, averaged over budgets, it leads there (0.297 against 0.272).
- The features that describe how the candidates diverge changed the logistic selector by less than 0.01.
- The headline table uses single-draw targets. With expected-gain targets and training on 510 seeds, the lead grows in four of six cells.

DEEP also reports that learned allocators capture more benefit than random but select nearly as much harm as random, so we report harm selected and normalized decision gain next to accuracy. The evaluation is open loop, and offline metrics correlate poorly with closed-loop results.

Calibrated accept, veto, replan and fallback decisions are later phases of CASS-VLA. They are not part of this claim. We expect to start from Localized Conformal Safety Monitoring and Look Again Before You Abstain, and we will describe them as contributions only after we build and test them.

## Relevance to the proposed method

CASS-VLA builds on prior work on decision-value triggers, trajectory critics and conformal monitoring. In the papers we found, none chooses among several perception tools from the divergence of a planner's candidate trajectories. The first phase targets that choice, with:

- trajectory-conditioned safety judgment;
- calibrated uncertainty estimation (later phase);
- selective active perception;
- runtime acceptance, veto, replanning, and fallback (later phase);
- explicit safety-versus-compute tradeoff analysis (later phase).
